Contracts, payslips, medical letters, ID scans: the PDFs people most need to merge, compress or sign are the ones they would least like a stranger to read. Yet the usual way to use an online PDF tool is to hand the file to it first. Before you do, seven questions will tell you most of what you need to know. None of them needs a lawyer, and you can answer most of them in a few minutes.

## 1. Where is the file processed?

This is the question everything else depends on. There are two basic designs:

- **On the website's servers.** Your file is uploaded, the work happens on the company's machines and the result is sent back. Most online PDF services work this way, and it is why many of them have a section about how long they keep your files.
- **In your browser.** The site sends you code, and the code does the work on your own device. The file is read from your disk into the page's memory and the result is built there too.

If a site says "secure" and "encrypted" but does not say which of these it is, assume the first.

## 2. If it is uploaded, how long is it kept?

A privacy policy or help page should give a number: minutes, hours, days. Look for the specific sentence about uploaded files, not the general promise to "take privacy seriously". A vague answer, or a retention period that applies only to some plans, is worth noting.

## 3. Who else receives the file or data about it?

Uploading to a PDF company can mean uploading to its hosting provider, its processing partners and anything else it lists as a sub-processor. Check the privacy policy for named providers. Also look at the page itself: advertising scripts, analytics and chat widgets are all third parties that load alongside your document.

## 4. Do I need an account?

An account is not a problem in itself, but it tells you what the site wants to know about you. A site that works with no account has less reason to tie your activity to an identity. When an account is needed, find out what it holds: an email address and a plan is a very different thing from a history of the documents you processed.

## 5. Does the marketing match the policy?

Phrases like "your files never leave your browser" are easy to write. Read the pricing page and the terms alongside the home page. If the home page says nothing is uploaded but the pricing page lists server-side features, one of them is wrong. For our own side-by-side look at this, see the [comparison pages](/compare), each of which lists the sources it was checked against.

## 6. Can I test the claim myself?

A claim you can check is worth more than one you cannot. Two checks work on almost any site:

1. **Watch the Network tab.** Open your browser's developer tools, select the Network tab, then use the tool. A tool that uploads your file shows a large request going out while the file is being processed.
2. **Go offline.** Load the tool, disconnect from Wi-Fi and use it again. A tool that has to upload your file cannot work without a connection.

The step-by-step version is in [how to check if a PDF site uploads your files](/guides/how-to-check-if-a-pdf-site-uploads-your-files), and our own live demonstration is at [See it for yourself](/verify).

## 7. What does the result carry with it?

Even when the processing is private, the file you get back may carry things you did not mean to share: author names, software details, dates and comments. A good tool tells you what it keeps and what it drops. Look for it on the tool's page, in the answers about what happens to bookmarks, form fields, metadata and links. If a tool does not say, test it: open the result's properties in two readers and look. [What is hidden inside a PDF](/guides/what-is-hidden-inside-a-pdf) explains what to look for.

## What these questions cannot tell you

Even a site that passes all seven cannot protect you from everything. Browser extensions can read any page you visit, including a document open in a tool. A device with malware is not safe whatever the website does. And a test shows what happened on your visit, not an audit of the company. Treat the questions as a way to rule out the worst designs and to learn how much trust you are being asked to give.

## A quick way to use this

For a one-off, low-stakes file, questions 1 and 6 are enough: where it is processed, and whether you can see it for yourself. For a document you would be unhappy to see published, go through all seven, and prefer a tool where the answer to the first question is "in your browser".
